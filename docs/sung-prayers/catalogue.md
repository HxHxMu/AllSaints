# All Saints: Sacred Texts for Original Music

**Research catalogue · 32 candidates · Advent 2026–Easter 2027**  
**Project:** All Saints — [allholysaints.com](https://allholysaints.com), YouTube `@AllHolySaints`  
**Creative direction:** Original contemplative “Gregorian jazz”: modal, spacious, chant-like vocals over jazz harmony. No reuse of existing melodies. Intended for YouTube devotional videos, not Mass.

For **All Saints**, build the repertoire around two complementary ideas: recognizable sacred poetry that helps people discover the channel, and historically authenticated prayers by saints whose voices are rarely heard today.

The most promising material includes **personal prayers, hymns, antiphons, and poetic meditations** written by canonized saints. Some originally had melodies; only the underlying words are proposed for new compositions.

> **Editorial warning:** Historical authorship, textual accuracy, and copyright clearance are three separate questions. A saint may have written a prayer while a popular English translation of it remains copyrighted. This is a research and production-planning catalogue, **not** a typeset collection of every complete original prayer or a final legal-clearance certificate.

## 1. Master catalogue

**Status:** **W** = written by the saint, with substantial historical evidence; **A** = attributed to the saint, not securely established; **E** = edited/adapted form of an authentic text.  
**PD English:** **Y** = an identifiable historical public-domain English rendering exists; **N** = an exact rendering was not sufficiently verified for clearance; **Partial** = historical translation covers only selected portions.  
**Priority:** **1** = leading production candidate; **2** = strong repertoire addition; **3** = research-sensitive or specialized.

Lengths are approximate: manuscripts and editions can vary in words, stanza divisions and lineation. An asterisk means the historical translation needs to be checked against the specific recension before publication.

**A note on the original texts:** The detailed entries below preserve original-language incipits, short verified quotations where available, and precise sources. They **do not** silently reconstruct entire longer texts, select a manuscript variant without labeling it, or present a paraphrase as the saint's words. Before recording, obtain the complete primary-source text from the edition identified and lock a final lyric master.

## 2. Detailed entries: Early Church, Celtic, Byzantine, and medieval saints

### 1. *Veni redemptor gentium* — St. Ambrose of Milan

**St. Ambrose (c. 339–397); feast: December 7; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin hymn beginning *Veni redemptor gentium, ostende partum Virginis*. Historically transmitted text normally has seven Ambrosian stanzas; some versions add a doxology which should not automatically be counted as Ambrose's own.
- **Evidence:** Early witnesses include Faustus of Riez, Cassiodorus and Pope Celestine I. Hymnological studies compare the manuscripts and distinguish later additions.
- **Primary text:** Early Latin witnesses include Düsseldorf MS B-3, fol. 269r; consult manuscript comparisons and the *Hymnology Archive* study.
- **Translations:** John Mason Neale, *The Hymnal Noted* (1851), English beginning *Come, thou Redeemer of the earth* — U.S. public domain. A specifically verified historical Spanish translation was not included.
- **Length and music:** Seven four-line stanzas, suitable intact for a measured 3–6 minutes; balanced, syllabic phrases. Pronunciation: *VEH-nee reh-DEM-ptor JEN-tee-oom*. Mood: expectancy, mystery, incarnation.
- **Fit:** Advent, especially December 7–24.
- **Comparisons:** Ambrosian/Sarum chant traditions; Luther's *Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland*; various Advent settings.

### 2. *Deus creator omnium* — St. Ambrose

**St. Ambrose (c. 339–397); feast: December 7; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin hymn beginning *Deus creator omnium, polique rector*. The received text has eight quatrains.
- **Evidence:** Augustine explicitly mentions this hymn in *Confessions* IX.12.32, providing strong contemporary support.
- **Primary text:** Ambrosian hymn corpus; Jacques Fontaine, *Ambroise de Milan: Hymnes* (1992), alongside Augustine's testimony.
- **Translations:** Multiple nineteenth-century English versions are documented in historical hymnology. A specific version must be checked against each stanza before clearance. No historical Spanish version independently cleared here.
- **Length and music:** Eight quatrains; the full hymn could last 4–5 minutes over a low pedal and restrained harmonic movement. Pronunciation: *DEH-oos kreh-AH-tor OM-nee-oom*. Mood: creation, sleep, evening trust.
- **Fit:** Vespers, evening or Compline-themed devotion.
- **Comparisons:** Historic plainsong and polyphonic settings.

### 3. *Sero te amavi* — St. Augustine of Hippo

**St. Augustine (354–430); feast: August 28; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin prose beginning *Sero te amavi, pulchritudo tam antiqua et tam nova*. A passage from *Confessiones* X.27.38, rather than a separately titled poem.
- **Primary text:** *Confessiones*, book X, chapter 27, paragraph 38; consult a CSEL critical text.
- **Translations:** Edward Bouverie Pusey, *The Confessions of St. Augustine* (1838), U.S. public domain. A modern English or Spanish devotional paraphrase should not be substituted without clearance.
- **Length and music:** Roughly 80 Latin words, appropriate intact for 3–5 minutes using parallel statements and repeated invocations. Pronunciation: *SEH-ro teh ah-MAH-vee*. Mood: longing, remorse, recognition, illumination.
- **Fit:** Lent, conversion, night prayer.
- **Comparisons:** Various choral and devotional adaptations of *Late Have I Loved You*. Compose a distinct melody and use a cleared text.

### 4. *Magnus es, Domine* — St. Augustine

**St. Augustine (354–430); feast: August 28; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin passage from *Confessiones* I.1.1, beginning *Magnus es, Domine, et laudabilis valde*; includes praise and the restlessness of the heart.
- **Primary text:** *Confessiones* I.1.1; CSEL 33, ed. Pius Knöll (1896).
- **Translations:** Pusey's nineteenth-century English translation (1838) is a U.S. public-domain option. Do not simply copy contemporary versions of the famous *cor inquietum* line.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 140 words. Choose the complete opening rhetorical movement through the heart's restlessness, or set the full passage for 5–6 minutes. Pronunciation: *MAHG-noos ess, DOH-mee-neh*. Mood: praise rising toward spiritual rest.
- **Fit:** Ordinary Time; Augustine's feast; conversion and discernment.
- **Comparisons:** Many musical and spoken-word works inspired by Augustine's opening.

### 5. Prayer for perseverance — St. Patrick

**St. Patrick (fifth century, died c. 461); feast: March 17; attribution: written by.**

This sentence comes from Patrick's authentic *Confessio*, unlike the historically uncertain *St. Patrick's Breastplate*.

**Complete selected original Latin sentence, *Confessio* §58:**

> Oro Deum ut det mihi perseuerantiam et dignetur ut reddam illi testem fidelem usque ad transitum meum propter Deum meum.

- **Primary text:** *Confessio* §58; the scholarly [St. Patrick's Confessio project](https://www.confessio.ie/) supplies textual and manuscript resources.
- **Translations:** Historical editions include Whitley Stokes's nineteenth-century work on Patrick; check the exact §58 text and its translation. Modern Pádraig McCarthy translation is not a public-domain substitute.
- **Length and music:** About 20–22 Latin words; one complete petition. A compact 3-minute piece with unaltered repetitions and instrumental space. Pronunciation: *OH-ro DEH-oom oot det MEE-hee pehr-seh-weh-RAHN-tsee-ahm*. Mood: courage and perseverance.
- **Fit:** March 17, Lent, missionaries, vocations.
- **Comparisons:** Few famous settings of this particular sentence.
- **Attribution caution:** *St. Patrick's Breastplate* (*Fáeth Fiada*) is traditionally associated with Patrick but not securely established as his composition.

### 6. Prayer of St. Ephrem — St. Ephrem the Syrian

**St. Ephrem (c. 306–373); Latin feast: June 9; attribution: attributed to, not securely written by.**

- **Original:** Received Greek prayer beginning *Κύριε καὶ Δέσποτα τῆς ζωῆς μου*, arranged in petitions rejecting vices, requesting virtues and asking to see one's own sins rather than judge others. Greek variants differ, including a petition concerning love of money.
- **Authorship:** The prayer is not in the securely authenticated Syriac corpus of Ephrem and is generally considered to reflect later monastic transmission. It is part of his **received liturgical legacy**, not securely his autograph composition. Consult Sebastian Brock's Ephrem scholarship and Byzantine liturgical text histories.
- **Primary text:** Greek Lenten prayer in the Byzantine Triodion, with textual variants.
- **Translations:** Contemporary liturgical versions, including late twentieth-century service-book translations, cannot be assumed public domain. No exact historical English/Spanish translation cleared here.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 60 Greek words; three complete petitions suit 3–5 minutes. Pronunciation: *KEE-ree-eh keh DES-po-ta tees zo-EES moo*. Mood: contrition, self-examination, hope.
- **Fit:** Byzantine Great Lent.
- **Comparisons:** Traditional Lenten recitation with prostrations, Eastern choral settings.
- **Label:** “Traditional Lenten Prayer Attributed to St. Ephrem.”

### 7. *Ἡ Παρθένος σήμερον* (*The Virgin Today*) — St. Romanos the Melodist

**St. Romanos (c. 490–556); feast: October 1; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Greek Nativity kontakion prooimion, beginning *Ἡ Παρθένος σήμερον, τὸν ὑπερούσιον τίκτει*. Evokes Mary, the cave, angels, shepherds, Magi and the birth of the pre-eternal God.
- **Primary text:** Greek Orthodox Archdiocese Nativity texts; Romanos's Nativity kontakion tradition. The familiar short excerpt is a **prooimion**, not the entire long kontakion.
- **Translations:** No specific historical public-domain English or Spanish rendering independently cleared here; modern liturgical translations need their own rights review.
- **Length and music:** Four to five short lines, roughly 40 Greek words. Excellent intact for 3–5 minutes. Pronunciation: *ee par-THEH-nos SEE-meh-ron*. Mood: warm, luminous, intimate, ancient.
- **Fit:** Christmas Eve/Christmas Day.
- **Comparisons:** Widely sung Byzantine third-tone settings; performances associated with Cappella Romana and Byzantine chant ensembles.

### 8. Paschal Canon, Ode 1 — St. John of Damascus

**St. John of Damascus (c. 675–749); feast: December 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Greek opening *Ἀναστάσεως ἡμέρα, λαμπρυνθῶμεν λαοί*, the first ode of the Paschal Canon. It celebrates the passage from death to life and earth to heaven.
- **Primary text:** Byzantine Pentecostarion, Paschal Canon, Ode 1, attributed within the received hymnographic tradition to St. John of Damascus.
- **Translations:** John Mason Neale, *Hymns of the Eastern Church* (1862), *The Day of Resurrection* or *'Tis the Day of Resurrection*, a public-domain poetic adaptation, **not** a word-for-word Greek translation.
- **Length and music:** The short ode, about 35 words, supports a spacious 3–5-minute setting. Pronunciation: *ah-nah-STAH-seh-os ee-MEH-rah*. Mood: resurrection, brightness, expansion.
- **Fit:** Easter Sunday and Easter season.
- **Comparisons:** Byzantine Paschal chant and the famous English Easter hymn from Neale's rendering.

### 9. Great Canon, opening penitential troparion — St. Andrew of Crete

**St. Andrew of Crete (c. 660–740); feast: July 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Greek troparion beginning *Πόθεν ἄρξομαι θρηνεῖν*, the first penitential troparion of Ode 1 following the heirmos. It asks where the soul should begin lamenting its past acts.
- **Primary text:** Greek Triodion; compare Sinai Greek manuscripts 734–735 and annotated scholarly Greek-English editions for variants.
- **Translations:** Historic Neale adaptations cover portions of the Great Canon, but the exact chosen troparion has **not** been matched to a verified public-domain translation here. Some scholarly English editions are contemporary and protected.
- **Length and music:** Short complete troparion, roughly 30 Greek words. Suitable as a 3-minute miniature; do not call it the whole Great Canon. Pronunciation: *POH-then AR-kho-mai three-NEEN*. Mood: honesty, remorse, searching.
- **Fit:** First and fifth weeks of Byzantine Lent.
- **Comparisons:** Monastic Great Canon recitation, heirmologic chant.

### 10. *Hymn of the Sinful Woman* — St. Kassia

**St. Kassia (c. 810–c. 865); Byzantine feast: September 7; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Greek troparion opening *Κύριε, ἡ ἐν πολλαῖς ἁμαρτίαις περιπεσοῦσα γυνή*. It gives voice to a woman anointing Christ's feet.
- **Evidence:** Part of the received Byzantine hymnographic corpus strongly associated with ninth-century composer Kassia. The popular legend involving Emperor Theophilos should not be used as proof of a coauthored line.
- **Primary text:** Greek Holy Week service-book/Triodion textual tradition; use a critical or carefully sourced edition to fix the exact recension.
- **Translations:** Exact historical public-domain English/Spanish versions not cleared. Commission a fresh rendering if recording in translation.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 100–150 words. Full text fits a dramatic 5–6-minute work with changes in address and intensity. Pronunciation: *KEE-ree-eh, ee en pol-LES ah-mar-TEE-ees*. Mood: nocturnal lament, contrition, mercy.
- **Fit:** Byzantine Holy Wednesday service sung Tuesday evening; useful as Holy Week devotional video for Latin Catholic viewers.
- **Comparisons:** Byzantine Kassia chant and long-form choral performances.
- **Caution:** Do not automatically conflate the anointing woman with Mary Magdalene or collapse distinct biblical narratives.

### 11. *O Pastor animarum* — St. Hildegard of Bingen

**St. Hildegard (1098–1179); feast: September 17; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin antiphon beginning *O Pastor animarum et O prima vox*.
- **Primary text:** *Symphonia*, Wiesbaden Riesencodex, fol. 466va. International Society of Hildegard von Bingen Studies transcription, drawing on Beverly Lomer and Barbara Newman's editions.
- **Translations:** Nathaniel M. Campbell's available English translation is modern and **not** established as public domain. No matched early English or Spanish version verified.
- **Length and music:** Five short poetic phrases, around 25–30 words. Full text makes an excellent 3–4-minute modal meditation. Pronunciation: *oh PAHS-tor ah-nee-MAH-room*. Mood: healing, refuge, quiet consolation.
- **Fit:** Lent, Ordinary Time, evening prayer.
- **Comparisons:** Hildegard's surviving original chant, from which the new composition must remain melodically independent.

### 12. *O virtus Sapientie* — St. Hildegard

**St. Hildegard (1098–1179); feast: September 17; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin antiphon beginning *O virtus Sapientie, que circuiens circuisti*; nine poetic lines.
- **Primary text:** *Symphonia*, Wiesbaden Riesencodex, fol. 466rb; see Barbara Newman, *Symphonia* (1988/1998) and the Hildegard studies transcription.
- **Translations:** Modern Nathaniel M. Campbell English is not automatically free to use; no verified pre-1931 matched version.
- **Length and music:** All nine lines fit 3–5 minutes; the imagery of Wisdom's three wings offers three instrumental responses. Pronunciation: *oh WEER-toos sah-pee-EN-tsee-eh*. Mood: ethereal, intellectual, luminous.
- **Fit:** Ordinary Time, Trinity reflection, September 17.
- **Comparisons:** Existing recordings of Hildegard's chant, whose distinctive melodic range is well known.

### 13. *O ignis Spiritus paracliti* — St. Hildegard

**St. Hildegard (1098–1179); feast: September 17; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin hymn beginning *O ignis Spiritus paracliti*.
- **Primary text:** Hildegard's *Symphonia*, preserved in the Dendermonde and Wiesbaden Riesencodex manuscript traditions.
- **Translations:** Richard Frederick Littledale, *O Fire of God the Comforter*, in *Lyra Messianica* (1864), is an identifiable public-domain English poetic rendering; check the actual printed stanza divisions. Spanish PD version not verified.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 150 words. Whole text suits 5–6 minutes; alternatively select one complete invocation without altering its address to the Holy Spirit. Pronunciation: *oh EE-nyees SPEE-ree-toos pah-rah-KLEE-tee*. Mood: warmth, flame, inspiration.
- **Fit:** Pentecost, Confirmation-themed devotion.
- **Comparisons:** Historical chant as recorded by medieval-music ensembles.

### 14. Prayer before the Crucifix — St. Francis of Assisi

**St. Francis (1181/82–1226); feast: October 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin recension beginning *Summe, gloriose Deus, illumina tenebras cordis mei*. An early Umbrian version begins *Altissimo glorioso Dio*. Do **not** blend recensions without labeling them.
- **Primary text:** Accepted authentic Franciscan writings, *Oratio ante Crucifixum* [OrCruc]; historically associated with the crucifix at San Damiano.
- **Translations:** Some popular online English versions are modern copyrighted Franciscan Institute renderings, including a 1999 translation. No precise historical English version is cleared in this catalogue.
- **Length and music:** About 30 words: requests for illumination, faith, hope, charity and discernment. A complete 3-minute miniature. Pronunciation: *SOOM-meh gloh-ree-OH-seh DEH-oos*. Mood: darkness yielding to light.
- **Fit:** Lent, Good Friday reflection, vocational discernment.
- **Comparisons:** Contemporary Franciscan devotional compositions.

### 15. *Laudes Dei Altissimi* — St. Francis of Assisi

**St. Francis (1181/82–1226); feast: October 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin litany beginning *Tu es sanctus, Dominus Deus solus*, using repeated *Tu es* to name divine attributes.
- **Evidence:** Exceptional: survives on the *Chartula*, the handwritten parchment Francis gave to Brother Leo, linked to Mount La Verna.
- **Primary text:** Francis's own *Chartula* and critical editions of *Laudes Dei Altissimi*.
- **Translations:** Paschal Robinson, *The Writings of Saint Francis of Assisi* (1905), contains historical English material; match the passage before using it. Spanish historical version not verified.
- **Length and music:** Roughly 110 words; a natural litany suitable intact for 4–6 minutes. Pronunciation: *too ess SAHNK-toos*. Mood: intimate reverence, awe.
- **Fit:** Ordinary Time, feast of St. Francis, Eucharistic adoration-themed personal prayer.
- **Comparisons:** Franciscan choir/chant traditions.

### 16. *Canticle of the Creatures* — St. Francis of Assisi

**St. Francis (1181/82–1226); feast: October 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Medieval Umbrian Italian, beginning *Laudato si', mi' Signore*, also *Canticum fratris Solis*.
- **Primary text:** Franciscan *Opuscula* [CantSol]; manuscript witness Assisi, Biblioteca Comunale, MS 338. Some stanzas were associated with distinct stages of the work's composition.
- **Translations:** Paschal Robinson, *The Writings of Saint Francis of Assisi* (1905), public-domain English option; verify exact wording and section order.
- **Length and music:** Around 200 words, with *Laudato si'* acting as a natural refrain. Full text might take 6–8 minutes. For a 3–6-minute excerpt, retain complete stanzas and make clear that the canticle is abridged; ideally preserve its arc through praise, forgiveness and death. Pronunciation: *low-DAH-toh see, mee see-NYOH-reh*. Mood: gratitude, creation, mortality.
- **Fit:** Easter season, creation-themed devotion, October 4.
- **Comparisons:** *All Creatures of Our God and King*—a later adaptation, not a literal reproduction of Francis's Umbrian.

### 17. *Benedictio Sanctae Clarae* — St. Clare of Assisi

**St. Clare (1194–1253); feast: August 11; attribution: written or dictated by.**

- **Original:** Latin blessing [BenCla] opening *In nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti*.
- **Primary text:** Clare's accepted writings, especially the *Benedictio* transmitted within the Franciscan corpus.
- **Evidence:** Authentic literary corpus, though not necessarily an autograph. Opening language draws on the priestly blessing in Numbers 6:24–26 and should be recognized as scriptural language included in Clare's composition.
- **Translations:** No exact public-domain full English or Spanish version verified; do not assume a modern Franciscan edition can be reused freely.
- **Length and music:** Over 150 words. Sing the complete opening blessing plus a thematically intact closing exhortation, labeled as an excerpt, or set the whole text for ~5–6 minutes. Pronunciation: *beh-neh-DEEK-tsee-oh KLAH-reh*. Mood: maternal tenderness and peace.
- **Fit:** August 11, religious vocations, Ordinary Time.
- **Comparisons:** Franciscan blessing traditions.

### 18. *Pange lingua gloriosi corporis mysterium* — St. Thomas Aquinas

**St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274); feast: January 28; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Latin Eucharistic hymn, six stanzas of six lines, composed for the Corpus Christi liturgical celebrations.
- **Primary text:** Cantus Index ID 830393; *Analecta Hymnica* 50, no. 386; medieval Dominican liturgical texts.
- **Translations:** Edward Caswall, *Lyra Catholica* (1849), beginning *Sing, my tongue, the Savior's glory*, public domain in the U.S.
- **Length and music:** Full six stanzas suitable for 5–6 minutes. Its final two stanzas, *Tantum ergo sacramentum*, are also a meaningful traditional unit suitable for a shorter work. Pronunciation: *PAHN-jeh LEEN-gwah gloh-ree-OH-see*. Mood: wonder, solemnity, Eucharistic reverence.
- **Fit:** Holy Thursday, Corpus Christi, Eucharistic devotion.
- **Comparisons:** Historical plainsong and numerous *Tantum ergo* settings, including concert/choral treatments.
- **Textual caution:** Do not confuse this with *Pange lingua gloriosi proelium certaminis* by St. Venantius Fortunatus.

### 19. *Per la Santificazione della Chiesa* (Prayer 20) — St. Catherine of Siena

**St. Catherine (1347–1380); feast: April 29; attribution: orally composed and recorded; substantially secure.**

- **Original:** Fourteenth-century Tuscan Italian opening *O Trinità etterna, Trinità etterna*.
- **Primary text:** *Le Orazioni*, no. 20, ed. Giuliana Cavallini, *Testi Cateriniani IV* (1978), with original vernacular and medieval Latin witnesses.
- **Evidence:** Catherine's prayers survive through manuscript recording by her disciples. Distinguish her oral composition from later editorial decisions and scribal readings.
- **Translations:** Suzanne Noffke, *The Prayers of Catherine of Siena* (1983 and later), is modern and copyrighted. Exact historical public-domain translation of Prayer 20 not verified.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 270 words. Take an intact opening paragraph or set all the words over a longer 6-minute structure. Pronunciation: *oh tree-nee-TAH eh-TEHR-nah*. Mood: ecstatic urgency, praise, intercession.
- **Fit:** Easter, Trinity Sunday, prayer for the Church.
- **Comparisons:** No dominant musical setting of this exact prayer identified.

### 20. *Nada te turbe* — St. Teresa of Ávila

**St. Teresa (1515–1582); feast: October 15; attribution: attributed to, not demonstrated.**

- **Original:** Spanish nine-line prayer/poem beginning *Nada te turbe, nada te espante*.
- **Evidence:** An association with a breviary belonging to Teresa is historically important but is **not proof of personal authorship**. The *Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology* and LiederNet preserve the attribution distinction.
- **Primary text:** Early Spanish manuscript and printed traditions; inspect exact recension before crediting.
- **Translations:** Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1871, beginning *Let nothing disturb thee*, a historical public-domain rendering.
- **Length and music:** Nine brief lines (~28 words). Naturally mantra-like and suitable intact for 3–5 minutes. Pronunciation: *NAH-dah teh TOOR-beh*. Mood: courage, calm, spiritual trust.
- **Fit:** Ordinary Time, night prayer, October 15.
- **Comparisons:** Jacques Berthier/Taizé's hugely familiar *Nada te turbe*. The historic words and the **protected modern Taizé melody/arrangement** are distinct rights.
- **Label:** “Traditional Prayer Attributed to St. Teresa of Ávila.”

### 21. *Vuestra soy, para Vos nací* — St. Teresa of Ávila

**St. Teresa (1515–1582); feast: October 15; attribution: written by, with literary dependence.**

- **Original:** Spanish poem with recurring refrain *Vuestra soy, para Vos nací: ¿qué mandáis hacer de mí?*
- **Evidence:** Transmitted within Teresa's poetic corpus, but literary scholarship by Rafael Lapesa traces its relation to an earlier secular villancico; Teresa contributed a religious adaptation of existing material.
- **Primary text:** Historical editions of Teresa's poetry, including P. Silverio de Santa Teresa (1919); compare a Carmelite Spanish original transcription.
- **Translations:** No exact public-domain English version established; the historical Spanish itself is the strongest musical choice.
- **Length and music:** Long poem. Use opening plus two or three **complete** stanzas, preserving the refrain, for 4–6 minutes. Pronunciation: *VWEHS-trah soy, PAH-rah vohs nah-SEE*. Mood: total surrender, joyful longing.
- **Fit:** Discernment, Ordinary Time, October 15.
- **Comparisons:** Many Spanish devotional songs built on the refrain.

### 22. *Llama de amor viva* — St. John of the Cross

**St. John of the Cross (1542–1591); feast: December 14; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Spanish mystical poem with four six-line stanzas, beginning *¡Oh llama de amor viva!* Two textual recensions are commonly distinguished as A and B.
- **Evidence:** John wrote the poem and his own theological commentary, giving strong confirmation of authorship. Choose a stated recension.
- **Primary text:** *Llama de amor viva*, poem and author's commentary, in a critical Spanish collected edition.
- **Translations:** David Lewis's nineteenth-century English St. John of the Cross translations, including *The Complete Works of Saint John of the Cross* (1864), offer a historical public-domain avenue, **subject to matching the exact stanza text**. Avoid twentieth-century modernizations.
- **Length and music:** Four stanzas, about 120 words, an excellent intact 4–6-minute composition. Pronunciation: *YAH-mah deh ah-MOR VEE-vah*. Mood: intimacy, fire, mystical union.
- **Fit:** Easter, Pentecost, December 14.
- **Comparisons:** Spanish Carmelite music and later choral settings.

### 23. *Suscipe* — St. Ignatius of Loyola

**St. Ignatius (1491–1556); feast: July 31; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** Spanish prayer from *Spiritual Exercises* §234, beginning *Tomad, Señor, y recibid toda mi libertad*. The **Spanish** is original; the familiar Latin is a translation.
- **Evidence:** Part of the authenticated *Exercises*; archival early Spanish and Latin codices include corrections and annotations linked to Ignatius.
- **Primary text:** *Ejercicios espirituales*, §234; Jesuit Roman Archives witness tradition.
- **Translations:** Elder Mullan, S.J., *The Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola* (1914), U.S. public-domain English candidate.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 60 words in an offering/acknowledgment/petition form. Full prayer suits 3–5 minutes. Pronunciation: *toh-MAHD, seh-NYOR, ee reh-thee-BEED*. Mood: freedom and surrender.
- **Fit:** Retreats, discernment, Ordinary Time.
- **Comparisons:** John Foley, S.J.'s *Take, Lord, Receive* and other protected contemporary settings.

### 24. Act of Spiritual Communion — St. Alphonsus Liguori

**St. Alphonsus (1696–1787); feast: August 1; attribution: written by; recension varies.**

- **Original:** Italian devotional text beginning *Gesù mio, credo che voi siete realmente presente*, traditionally associated with *Visite al Santissimo Sacramento* (1745). Editions vary in spelling, endings and added aspirations.
- **Primary text:** Early Italian editions of Alphonsus's Eucharistic visits and prayers; obtain an early recension and use it consistently.
- **Translations:** An English form appears in *Blessed Be God* (1925), p. 321, public domain in the U.S.; its **additional concluding aspiration** means it may not be a literal translation of one short Italian original.
- **Length and music:** About 60–80 words depending on recension. Natural three-part shape: belief, desire, spiritual union. Pronunciation: *jeh-ZOO mee-oh, KREH-doh*. Mood: intimacy and reverence.
- **Fit:** Corpus Christi, Eucharistic devotion, inability to receive sacramentally.
- **Comparisons:** Familiar spoken devotional versions and newer songs.
- **Caution:** A spiritual communion should not be presented as equivalent to sacramental Eucharistic reception.

### 25. *Lead, Kindly Light* — St. John Henry Newman

**St. John Henry Newman (1801–1890); feast: October 9; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** English poem written in 1833 as *The Pillar of the Cloud*, later known as *Lead, Kindly Light*.
- **Primary text:** Newman's original three-stanza poem and early printings.
- **Translation/copyright:** Original English is public domain in the U.S. Historical Spanish translations require separate checks.
- **Textual issue:** A fourth stanza beginning *Meanwhile, along the narrow...* was added by Edward Henry Bickersteth, **not Newman**; Newman disputed the attribution. Use only the original three stanzas.
- **Length and music:** Three six-line stanzas; complete in ~4–5 minutes. English voice delivery should keep line endings audible. Mood: night, uncertainty, guiding light.
- **Fit:** Advent, evening, discernment.
- **Comparisons:** Historic hymn tunes *Lux Benigna*, *Sandon*, and many choral arrangements.

### 26. *Vivre d'amour* — St. Thérèse of Lisieux

**St. Thérèse (1873–1897); feast: October 1; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** French poem PN17, *Vivre d'amour*, composed in February 1895, around fifteen stanzas.
- **Primary text:** Thérèse's poetic corpus in the Lisieux Carmelite archives; compare early printings with autograph/critical editions since some posthumous texts were edited.
- **Translations:** Susan L. Emery, *Poems of Sr. Teresa, Carmelite of Lisieux* (Boston, 1907), a historical English volume in the U.S. public domain. Match title, individual stanza order and wording against the digitization before use.
- **Length and music:** Too long for one short song intact. Select two or three complete stanzas, preserving any authentic refrain; identify the selection as an excerpt. Pronunciation: *VEE-vruh dah-MOOR*. Mood: joyful surrender and luminous love.
- **Fit:** Easter, October 1.
- **Comparisons:** Modern French recordings associated with *Thérèse: Vivre d'amour*, including Natasha St-Pier performances.

### 27. *Ô mon Dieu, Trinité que j'adore* — St. Elizabeth of the Trinity

**St. Elizabeth of the Trinity (1880–1906); feast: November 8; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** French prayer written November 21, 1904, beginning *Ô mon Dieu, Trinité que j'adore*.
- **Primary text:** Elizabeth's spiritual writings and prayer notes; Carmel Notre-Dame de Surieu supplies a dated French transcription.
- **Translations:** No exact pre-1931 English or Spanish edition independently cleared; modern English quotations are protected unless released by their authors or rights holders.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 300 words in sequential invocations to the Trinity, Christ, Word and Holy Spirit. The first complete invocation is a strong self-contained 4–5-minute setting; a full piece would require greater length. Pronunciation: *oh mohn DYUH, tree-nee-TAY kuh zhah-DOR*. Mood: silence, interior vastness, adoration.
- **Fit:** Trinity Sunday, November 8, adoration-themed devotion.
- **Comparisons:** French Carmelite devotional recordings, with no single globally dominant setting of this exact text.

### 28. Meditation on abandonment — St. Charles de Foucauld

**St. Charles de Foucauld (1858–1916); feast: December 1; attribution: original meditation written by; short familiar prayer is edited.**

- **Original:** French 1896 meditation on Luke 23:46, containing *Mon Père, je me remets entre Vos mains*.
- **Evidence:** The spiritual family of Charles de Foucauld and the Diocese of Paris explain that the widely known *Prière d'abandon* was shortened/edited from a larger authentic meditation. It must not be presented as his exact unmodified text.
- **Primary text:** Unabridged 1896 meditation, in editions and archival resources of the Charles de Foucauld spiritual family; Little Sisters of Jesus reproduce the longer meditation beside the familiar adaptation.
- **Translations:** No verified public-domain English translation of the **unabridged** original. Contemporary translations of the edited prayer are not safe stand-ins.
- **Length and music:** From the meditation, choose a complete accurately transcribed paragraph with its repeated address to the Father, clearly labeled as an excerpt. Pronunciation: *mohn PEHR, zhuh muh ruh-MEH ahn-truh voh MEHN*. Mood: extreme trust and surrender.
- **Fit:** Lent, Good Friday, December 1.
- **Comparisons:** Numerous familiar recordings of the modern shortened *Prayer of Abandonment*.

### 29. *Esprit Saint, inspire-moi* — St. Mariam Baouardy

**St. Mariam Baouardy, Mary of Jesus Crucified (1846–1878); feast: August 26; attribution: written by, pending archival confirmation of reproduced handwriting.**

- **Original:** French nine-line prayer opening *Esprit Saint, inspire-moi*.
- **Evidence:** A published musical resource reproduces what is identified as a handwritten version signed *Sr Marie de Jésus crucifié*. The image is stronger evidence than an unattributed prayer page, but archival provenance of the handwriting still needs confirmation.
- **Primary text:** Signed handwriting as reproduced in a French devotional music sheet; trace it back to the archival original before treating the modern transcription as definitive.
- **Translations:** No historically verified public-domain English or Spanish version; commission a new translation.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 30–40 words, invoking the Holy Spirit, love, Mary, Jesus and protection from error or evil. Whole short poem can sustain 3 minutes. Pronunciation: *es-PREE SAHN, an-SPEER MWAH*. Mood: childlike prayer, mystery, protection.
- **Fit:** Pentecost, August 26, morning prayer.
- **Comparisons:** French musical settings; note that one sheet adds a threefold *Amen* **by the composer**, not the saint.

### 30. *Prière d'une pauvre mendiante à Jésus* — St. Bernadette Soubirous

**St. Bernadette (1844–1879); feast: April 16; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** French notebook prayer opening *Ô Jésus, donnez-moi, je vous prie, le pain de l'humilité*. Each petition uses the metaphor of bread.
- **Primary text:** *Carnet de notes intimes*, in critical editions of Bernadette's writings; a Catholic educational transcription connects the prayer to the notebook.
- **Translations:** No exact early public-domain English or Spanish version verified. Contemporary Lourdes-related renderings may be protected.
- **Length and music:** Approximately 75–90 words. Repeated *le pain de...* phrases create a natural litany for 3–5 minutes. Pronunciation: *oh zhay-ZOO, doh-NAY mwah*. Mood: humility and endurance.
- **Fit:** Lent, April 16, prayer during suffering.
- **Comparisons:** French Lourdes devotional music; no single famous setting of the exact notebook prayer dominates.

**St. Elizabeth Ann Seton (1774–1821); feast: January 4; attribution: written by.**

- **Original:** English prayer beginning *O Blessed Jesus, my tender Redeemer, My Merciful Lord*. The text petitions Christ for faith, hope, charity and perseverance.
- **Primary text:** *Elizabeth Bayley Seton: Collected Writings*, vol. IIIb, ed. Regina Bechtle and Judith Metz (New City Press, 2006), item **11.39**, pp. **56–57**; archival identifier **ASJPH 1-3-3-3:82**.

### 32. Martyrdom poem to the Sacred Heart — St. José María Robles Hurtado

**St. José María Robles Hurtado (1888–1927), Mexican priest and martyr; feast: June 26; attribution: written by, complete text unverified.**

- **Original:** Spanish devotional poem beginning *Quiero amar tu Corazón, Jesús mío, con delirio*.
- **Evidence:** The Vatican's official canonization biography states that he wrote a poem shortly before being executed on June 26, 1927, and reproduces part of it. This is strong evidence **for the quoted excerpt**, not proof of any unlocated full transcription.
- **Primary text:** Vatican canonization biography for St. José María Robles Hurtado (canonized May 21, 2000). Full contemporary manuscript or critical edition **not located**.
- **Copyright:** The original historical words are outside ordinary modern U.S. author-term protection; later published editions and translations need checking. No verified public-domain English version.
- **Length and music:** **Verified excerpt only; full text unverified.** Do not call the excerpt the complete poem. Pronunciation: *KYEH-roh ah-MAHR too koh-rah-SOHN*. Mood: sacrifice, ardor, courage.
- **Fit:** June 26, Sacred Heart, Mexican martyrs.
- **Comparisons:** Traditional Mexican Sacred Heart hymnody.
- **Production status:** **Hold for source work** if the intended composition needs the full poem.

**Additional research lead:** St. Rose of Lima's authentic mystical writings, including illustrated *Mercedes y escala mística*, warrant further paleographic study. Do not invent a prayer by converting her symbolic illustrations into supposedly verbatim lyrics.

## 4. Famous prayers to exclude or relabel

| Familiar prayer | Accurate editorial treatment |
|---|---|
| “Lord, make me an instrument of your peace” | **Misattributed to Francis of Assisi.** The modern Peace Prayer first appeared anonymously in French in **1912**, centuries after Francis. |
| *St. Patrick's Breastplate* | Traditional Irish hymn associated with Patrick, but not securely written by him. Label “Traditional Irish prayer attributed to St. Patrick.” |
| *Anima Christi* | Medieval text predating Ignatius of Loyola. Its use in Jesuit spirituality does not establish Ignatian authorship. |
| *O Deus ego amo te* / “My God, I love Thee” | Often linked to Francis Xavier; historical attribution and language transmission disputed. Require a particular critical edition. |
| *Nada te turbe* | Associated with Teresa of Ávila's breviary, but not demonstrably composed by her. |
| Familiar short *Prayer of Abandonment* | Based on Foucauld's 1896 meditation but subsequently excerpted and edited. |

The purpose is not to reject traditional devotional treasures; it is to separate **traditional association** from **verifiable personal authorship**, especially because authorship is central to the All Saints identity.

## 5. Musical and pronunciation guidance

For the Gregorian-jazz concept, the strongest resource is the **contrast between unadorned recitative and spacious harmony**, rather than imitation of surviving chant melodies.

| Language | Pronunciation approach | Musical implications |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | Ecclesiastical Latin; clear vowels; soft *c* before *e/i* | Pedal tones, monophonic declamation, restrained modal voicings |
| Medieval Umbrian/Italian | Italianate vowels; check archaic spelling | Refrains, melodic repetition, expressive syllabic singing |
| Spanish | Five clear vowels; authentic spoken word stress | Lyrical refrains and direct, intimate vocal delivery |
| Byzantine Greek | Select a historically appropriate or modern liturgical pronunciation and use it consistently | Preserve accentual rhythm; avoid forcing English hymn meter |
| French | Work with a fluent coach for vowels, liaison and final consonants | Sustained notes, shimmering harmonic space |
| Old Irish / Celtic | Specialist language coach for actual Old Irish originals | Avoid invented pronunciation from English hymn titles |
| Syriac | Syriac liturgical or scholarly coach | A wonderful repertoire opportunity, but do not reconstruct Syriac lyrics from a doubtful Greek attribution |

Suggested shared arrangement vocabulary: solo unison voice; occasional parallel fourths/fifths; a low sustained drone; subtle harmonic changes at theological pivots; an ensemble entering only on the final petition or doxology. Use jazz in the voicings, timbre, phrasing, suspension and open resolutions rather than continuous chord changes.

A 25-word prayer can genuinely support 3–6 minutes by repeating **complete, intact textual units** with space for instrumental meditation. A 300-word prayer usually needs a longer setting or a clearly identified, semantically complete excerpt.

## 6. Catholic guidance for non-liturgical devotional music

These videos are **devotional works, not musical settings being inserted into the Mass**. The distinction is essential.

- Vatican II, *Sacrosanctum Concilium* §§112–121: sacred music is important to the liturgy; Gregorian chant has a privileged place in the Roman Rite; new sacred compositions are also encouraged.
- *Musicam sacram* (1967), especially §9: musical suitability for actual worship relates to sacred character and participation.
- Vatican, *Directory on Popular Piety and the Liturgy* (2002), especially §§13, 16–17: distinguishes devotional practices from liturgical rites, insists on doctrinal fidelity, and provides room for new devotional songs.

None of these texts creates a categorical ban on jazz-inflected harmony in **non-liturgical** devotional listening. Artistic freedom still carries responsibilities of accuracy and reverence:

1. **Preserve the saint's words.** Repetitions are a compositional choice; newly written lines must never be falsely quoted as the saint's own.
2. **Avoid liturgical misrepresentation.** Market as an original contemplative devotional setting, not historical Gregorian chant or an officially approved liturgical composition.
3. **Protect theological meaning.** An excerpt should not remove the passage's essential movement toward repentance, mercy, hope or praise.
4. **Respect sacred words and language.** Have unfamiliar Greek, medieval vernacular and French texts reviewed by competent readers.
5. **Honor Eastern distinctiveness.** Kassia, Romanos and John of Damascus are part of Byzantine hymnography; do not subsume them under Western Gregorian history.

The sound can be innovative while retaining discernible Catholic devotion: strong provenance, carefully articulated vocals and visual restraint matter more than whether a harmony includes ninths or quartal structures.

## 7. Copyright and publishing on YouTube

**YouTube-only release is still audiovisual publication.** Avoiding Spotify/streaming does not eliminate rights issues for translations, recordings, arrangements or incorporated audiovisual materials.

### 7.1 Public-domain original vs. translation

Ancient and medieval saints' original texts are generally public domain in the United States. Works published in the United States before **January 1, 1931**, are ordinarily in the public domain as of 2026. Foreign works and unpublished manuscripts have specific rules; the publication cutoff is **not universal**.

A modern translation of a fourth-century Latin prayer can remain copyrighted. Examples to **check or avoid without authorization** include modern ICEL liturgical translations, NABRE Scripture renderings, Suzanne Noffke's English Catherine of Siena prayers, and contemporary Franciscan or Carmelite editions. A public-domain poem, a modern arrangement of it and a recording of that arrangement are legally distinct works.

### 7.2 Write or commission an original translation

If translating directly from a public-domain original, your own independent translation can contain protectable modern expression. Do not merely rewrite a copyrighted translation line by line.

If commissioning a translator, use a signed written agreement. Depending on circumstances, a commissioned translation may qualify as a U.S. **work made for hire** only if statutory requirements—including the appropriate written agreement—are satisfied; otherwise use a written copyright assignment or sufficiently broad exclusive license.

The contract should address: sung lyrics, subtitles, lyric videos, promo clips, derivative editions, monetized global YouTube use, optional future streaming use, adaptation rights, warranty of independent authorship, crediting terms and which party owns the final translation.

### 7.3 Protect your original composition and master

Your independently composed melody, harmonic treatment, arrangement and sound recording can carry separate rights. Secure appropriate written permissions from session singers, performers, arrangers and other contributors; payment alone does not settle every ownership issue.

Retain the following production evidence:

- Original-language primary source and exact edition/manuscript description.
- Final lyric master with punctuation, repeated text and stanza order.
- Translation source and public-domain proof, or translator agreement.
- Composition/session files establishing your independently created melody and arrangement.
- Signed performer and master-ownership documents.
- Final video, metadata and credits.

A Content ID claim may still occur for original music; these records support a challenge. Do not claim exclusive copyright in the ancient underlying words themselves.
